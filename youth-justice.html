For professionals · Youth justice

Keeping children out of the justice system, and helping those already in it.

Onesimus works mainly with adults in the justice system and their families. Youth justice is part of the same picture. This section gathers practical, evidence-informed material on youth justice for practitioners, policy makers and anyone working with young people in Tasmania.

Why Onesimus is involved

The adult system starts with the youth system.

Onesimus works where the justice system meets families and communities. Youth justice sits squarely in that space: the earlier a young person is drawn into custody, the harder the road back tends to be, for them and for the people around them.

We share this material to support informed discussion among practitioners. We are grateful to Professor Rob White for making his work available here.

Why these sources give different numbers

All three sources are official, current and correct. They disagree because they count different things, and knowing which question you are asking decides which one to use.

The same question, asked two ways: how many young Tasmanians are under supervision on an average day?

110

ROGS: young people aged 10–17 only.

163

AIHW: everyone under youth justice supervision, including about 52 aged 18 and over who entered the system as children.

Neither is wrong. They draw the age line in different places, so always check which age range a youth figure covers.

ROGS is for comparing — mostly. It provides a common framework for comparing youth justice across the states: what detention costs, how full it is, how many young people come back. Published each January. But the completeness and comparability of individual measures vary, so always check the qualifications on each answer.

AIHW is for detail. It carries what the others leave out — how many are on remand, how old they are, how long supervision lasts, and how many pass through in a year.

ABS casts the widest net. It counts every young person police formally dealt with across the year. Most of them never come under youth justice supervision at all.

Small numbers jump about. Tasmania holds about fifteen young people in detention on an average night. That matters most for detention and for Aboriginal young people in detention, where one or two young people either way can move a percentage or ratio by several points. The overall supervision rate, built on about 110 young people, moves much less.

Average day or whole year? About 163 young people are under supervision on an average day, but 266 were supervised at some point during 2024–25. Check which one a figure counts before comparing it with another.

Tasmania's twelve-month orders. The AIHW notes that Tasmanian courts generally set sentenced youth community orders to default to twelve months. Longer supervision periods may contribute to higher average daily supervision numbers, but how much they contribute cannot be established from these figures alone.
