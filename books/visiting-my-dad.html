Visiting My Dad in Prison

Charlie and Bessy Bear go to Risdon

A picture book for younger children about what actually happens when you visit someone in prison — the waiting, the lockers, the search, the dog, the loud doors, and seeing Dad. The photographs were taken inside the Risdon Prison Complex.

It works two ways: read it before a visit so a child knows what is coming, or read it afterwards to help them make sense of what happened. Use the switch at the top to change which questions you see.

Some details have changed. The photographs were taken in 2018 and a few things at the prison are now different. Where that matters, we have said so on the page rather than altering the book. The feelings in it have not dated at all.
This has changed since the photographs were taken

The machine in this picture is no longer used. Today an adult visitor has a scan of their palm and a scan of their eye, taken the first time they visit and then every visit after that. Children are not scanned at all — they come in with the adult.

Stop and talk

Froggie had to stay behind.

  • Charlie could not take Froggie in. What would you like to bring, and what might have to wait in the locker?
  • Is there something you would rather leave in the car, so it does not have to go in a locker?

Did you have to put things away?

  • Was there something you had to leave in the locker?
  • How did that feel? Did you get it back at the end?

For the adultChildren often fix on the one thing that was taken from them rather than the whole search. Naming it beforehand, and naming where it will be waiting, takes most of the sting out of it.

Stop and talk

Some parts are loud.

  • The big doors bang. What helps you when something is loud or sudden?
  • Who will be with you if you get a fright?

Was anything louder than you expected?

  • Was there a noise that gave you a fright?
  • What helped?

For the adultChildren rarely volunteer fear when asked about it directly. Asking about noise is an easier door in than asking about feelings, and it usually leads to the same place.

Stop and talk

What will you tell Dad about?

  • Charlie told his Dad about day care and about helping clean his room. What will you tell yours?
  • Is there something you could bring to talk about?

What did you tell him?

  • What did you talk about?
  • Is there something you forgot, that you could save for next time?

For the adultA child who arrives with something to say has a job to do, which is steadier than arriving with only feelings to manage. Saving something for next time also gives the next visit a purpose rather than only a wait.

Stop and talk

The goodbye comes quickly.

  • Visiting time goes fast and then it is time to go. What would you like to do when you wave goodbye?
  • Would you like a special wave, or a word that is just yours and Dad's?

Goodbyes are the hardest part.

  • How was saying goodbye?
  • What would make the next one easier?

For the adultThe goodbye is where most visits come undone. A small ritual the child owns gives them something to do at the moment they feel most helpless, and something to look forward to repeating.

Before you go

Two things turn families away at the gate: arriving less than 30 minutes before the visit time, and not bringing identification for every child. A Medicare card, a Healthcare or Concession card, or a birth certificate.

There is a checklist for a first visit, and the rest of the practical detail, in our phone guide.
