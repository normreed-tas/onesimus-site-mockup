Understanding the impact of offending on the whole family

Draft for reviewNot final. The purple notes mark points still to confirm or verify. Each section ends with the 2018 guide's own wording.

  • 3.1 Impact on parents and carers
  • 3.2 Impact on children and young people

"It's not just the prisoner that's punished is it? It's the whole family."

Mother, quoted in the 2018 guide

You can't support a family well without some idea of what offending has done to it. This chapter sets out what that can look like for the adults caring for the children (3.1) and for the children themselves (3.2).

Two things to hold on to:

  • Every family member is affected differently, and the effect changes over time. That's why this guide takes a whole-family approach.
  • Prison isn't always only a loss. If the person was violent, or their life was chaotic, their absence can bring relief. Children can feel relief and loss at the same time, and feel guilty about the relief.

How offending gets in the way of children's needs

In the 1940s the psychologist Abraham Maslow described human needs as a pyramid. Our most basic needs, like food, sleep and safety, have to be met before we can do much about the ones above them. The 2018 guide used his pyramid to show how a family member's offending can affect a family's ability to meet children's needs at every level.

NeedWhat it coversHow offending can affect it
BodyFood, sleep, warmthFamilies can slide into poverty, which affects food and quality of life. Stress and worry disturb sleep.
SafetySecurity of home, income, healthSome offences bring threats against the family. Routines, childcare and getting to work are disrupted. Families face a higher risk of poor physical and mental health, and of losing their housing.
BelongingRelationshipsA key attachment figure is gone. Children may cut themselves off from friends, become secretive, or be bullied. Care can become inconsistent, and the whole family's way of working changes.
EsteemConfidence, respect, achievementStigma can lower children's chances and confidence. Some children start to see themselves as "bad too".
Reaching potentialGrowing into who they could beChildren may come to reject people in authority. Secrets and lies can blur what's true. When the needs below aren't met, children can't reach their full potential.

Other needs and problems the family already had, such as money trouble or poor mental health, can get worse too.

Safeguarding: think about where these needs stand for the child in front of you. One unmet need, or a combination, may mean a safeguarding referral (Chapter 2.4).

3.1 Impact on parents and carers

"I really miss him. I'm always in tears. He's missing so much of (son)… I find it really sad… (Partner) was a really good Dad. He did half of it all. I find it really hard."

Mother, quoted in the 2018 guide

When someone goes to prison, the people left caring for the children face a new set of problems. A partner may suddenly be raising children alone. Grandparents or other relatives may take the children in, and their own lives change a great deal. Carers have to manage the practical and emotional fallout for themselves and for the children at the same time.

How many families? New figures

  • 1 in 3 people entering prison (34%) have children in the community who depend on them for their basic needs. 1 in 5 (21%) had a parent or carer in prison when they were a child (AIHW, The health of people in Australia's prisons 2025; Australia-wide).
  • More than 40,000 children in Australia have a parent in prison. This is a research estimate, not a count (Monash University for SHINE for Kids, 2023).
  • Nobody counts these children. Prisons don't routinely record whether the people they receive have children, so there is no official figure for Australia or for Tasmania.
  • Tasmania's prisons are fuller than ever. On an average day in 2025–26 they held 845 people, up 7.9% on the year before, with a new high of 882 in April 2026 (Department of Justice Annual Report 2025–26). On the ABS count for 30 June 2025, 68 were women, up 11% on the year (ABS, Prisoners in Australia 2025).

The sources and checks for every figure are on the site's Tasmanian justice statistics page.

What parents and carers tell us they face

  • Money: lost income, rent arrears, Centrelink claims that are hard to understand
  • Pressure from the person in prison to send them money
  • Everyone knowing, because it was in the news
  • Children in trouble at school, not sleeping, anxious or clingy
  • Hearing stories about violence in prison
  • Coping alone, with no support
  • Other family members not speaking to them
  • Feeling cut off from their community
  • Long trips to visit: Risdon Prison and the Mary Hutchinson Women's Prison are near Hobart, a long way from the north and north-west

Chapter 4.1 covers how to assess these needs and make referrals. Services for each one are in the Field Guide and the Tasmanian referral list.

Why needs differ from family to family

How hard it hits a parent or carer depends on:

  • their emotional resilience and their physical and mental health
  • their culture and community
  • their relationship with the children, and their parenting skills
  • the family and community support around them
  • their relationship with the person who offended
  • how much childcare they have to do
  • how independent they are, with money and day-to-day things
  • what the offence was
  • where the person is up to in the justice system (Chapter 2.2)
  • media coverage, and how the community responds

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families New figures

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults in Tasmania are imprisoned at 6.7 times the rate of other Tasmanians. That is the lowest gap in the country, but it has widened since 2021–22 (Report on Government Services 2026). The ABS counted 206 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in Tasmanian prisons on 30 June 2025 (ABS 2025). Among First Nations people entering prison nationally, 31% had a parent or carer in prison when they were a child (AIHW 2025). verify: not yet on the site's figures register

Think about how culture, community and family obligations shape what a family's experience is, and what support will feel safe to them. Ask whether they'd like an Aboriginal organisation involved. 13YARN and the Tasmanian Aboriginal Legal Service are in the Field Guide.

Release can be hard too

Each stage of the justice system brings its own pressures (Chapter 2.2). Release isn't always a relief. Some families find the person coming home a source of real anxiety, especially if things weren't safe before.

3.2 Impact on children and young people

"I'm so angry because you promised you would come back and take us and we would live with you. You left me having nightmares. I didn't know if you were safe, and I was really scared."

Young person, quoted in the 2018 guide

How a child is affected depends on:

  • their age
  • whether the person is a key attachment figure, someone they depend on for care and comfort
  • what the offence was
  • their resilience

A child's resilience, their ability to cope, depends in turn on:

  • their own nature
  • how stable the family is
  • the quality of their other close relationships, such as with brothers and sisters
  • whether they can keep up a relationship with the person in prison
  • whether they get the chance to talk openly about what's happening (Jones et al. 2013)

It changes over time

The impact rises and falls with events. A child might be thinking:

  • My brother was arrested last night… the police came to our house…
  • We're going to visit the prison tomorrow…
  • My brother's coming home to visit for a day… he'll be out soon…
  • He won't be here for my birthday this year…

Watch for other moments that can trigger feelings or behaviour, such as parent–teacher interviews, Father's Day or Mother's Day at school, court dates, and news reports.

Changes at home

As the person moves through the system, from arrest to prison to release, a child's life can change in many ways:

  • A different carer.
  • A new house, school or community. Some families move because of the offence, because money is short, or so the child can live with another carer.
  • A different routine, for example when a carer takes on more work to make ends meet.
  • Different relationships. Friends may treat the child differently. Brothers and sisters may react differently from each other, and that can come between them.
  • Less money.
  • A different role in the family. Older children sometimes end up caring for younger ones, or for a parent who isn't coping.
  • A carer who is upset or acting differently. Children may not realise that's because of the offending.

When a mother or main carer goes to prison

When the person in prison is a child's mother or main carer, the child is more likely to change carer, home and school. Think about this when you assess what the family needs (Chapter 4.1).

In Tasmania, women are held at the Mary Hutchinson Women's Prison, part of the Risdon Prison Complex at Risdon Vale. On 30 June 2025 there were 68 women in Tasmanian prisons (ABS).

Babies and young children living with their mother. The prison has a mother–baby unit. A child up to two years old may live there with their mother if TPS assesses that it is safe and appropriate.

Visits, phone calls and letters work as for the men's prisons. See Visiting and Contact on the TPS site.

Child protection New figures

In 2023–24, about 179,000 Australian children, around 1 in 31, came into contact with the child protection system (AIHW, Child protection Australia 2023–24). The 2018 guide's figure was 1 in 35, for 2014–15. verify: not yet on the site's figures register

Disrupted attachment

"Parent–child relationships are undermined, disrupted and damaged by the court and prison systems."

Galloway et al. 2014

Babies and young children need warm, responsive and consistent care. When the comfort they reach for is patchy or missing, their attachment can become poor or disorganised, and that can harm their development. Children of people who offend are particularly at risk.

Prison removes a parent altogether. But attachment can also be damaged by inconsistent care around the offending, by restricted contact, or by confused stories about where the person is (Murray & Murray 2010).

Think about the other ways offending can affect attachment. For example: a carer stretched thin by coping alone, or a child whose carer keeps changing as the person goes in and out of prison.

Loss and grief

"There is no closure in these cases; the challenge is in how to live with the ambiguity."

Jones et al. 2013

Researchers describe what children go through in two ways.

Ambiguous loss. The person is physically gone but still very present in the child's mind, as in a divorce. Children often don't know or understand how long it will be until the person comes back. It's even less clear when they haven't been told the truth about where the person is (Bocknek et al. 2009; Jones et al. 2013). Children who can make some sense of the separation, and keep hope, may cope better. Being open with them about prison helps with that (Jones et al. 2013).

Disenfranchised grief. This is a real loss that people can't openly mourn. Because prison carries a stigma, the child's loss isn't "socially supported": people don't bring a casserole or send a card. It has been compared to a death without the chance to mourn (Hames & Pedreira 2003).

Like children who've had someone die, children with a family member in prison need help to:

  • accept the loss
  • feel the pain of it
  • adjust to life without the person at home
  • remember the person (Hames & Pedreira 2003)

Chapter 5 has practical ways to help, and the tools in the Field Guide under Tools for talking with children.

Signs to watch for

These vary from child to child, but may include:

  • moodiness, aggression, or chattering more than usual
  • bullying, or trouble getting on with other children
  • seeming withdrawn
  • poor concentration or tiredness
  • losing interest in schoolwork
  • hostility towards teachers and other people in authority
  • bedwetting
  • separation anxiety, such as not wanting to leave a carer
  • using alcohol or other drugs
  • self-harm, or talk of suicide

If a child talks about self-harm or suicide, act on it that day. Follow your organisation's procedure. If they're in danger now, call 000. Access Mental Health, Kids Helpline and the Strong Families, Safe Kids Advice and Referral Line are under Help right now in the Field Guide. New
