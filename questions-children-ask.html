The questions children ask

Children ask the hardest questions at the worst moments: in the car, at bedtime, in the shop.

You don't need a perfect answer. You need a short, true one. If you get caught out, you can say: "That's a really good question. Let me think, and I'll tell you after tea." Then do.

Swap in Mum, Pop, your sister, or whoever it is.

Is it my fault?

You could say: "No. Nothing you did, or said, or thought made this happen. This is about a choice a grown-up made."

What may be behind it: Many children quietly decide they caused it: by being naughty, by a fight they remember, by something they wished. They may never ask out loud. Say this one even if they don't ask, and say it again.

When is he coming home?

You could say:

  • If you know: "Not for a long time. After your next birthday, and the one after that." Use birthdays, Christmases or school terms for young children, not months or years.
  • If you don't know: "I don't know yet. The court decides. I will tell you as soon as I know."

Don't say "soon" unless it is. Dates change, and a broken promise hurts more than "I don't know".

What may be behind it: "Will things ever be normal again?" and "Who looks after me until then?" Tell them what stays the same.

For some children, the person coming home is a frightening thought. If your child goes quiet when you talk about it, ask gently: "How do you feel about Dad coming home one day?"

Where is he? What is it like there?

You could say: "He is in a prison. In Tasmania most people are at Risdon, near Hobart. He has a bed, meals, and things to do in the day. There are officers whose job is to keep everyone safe."

What may be behind it: "Is he okay? Is he scared? Is he being hurt?" Children get their picture of prison from TV. If you have spoken to him, tell them something ordinary he said: what he had for tea, what he is reading.

Is he in a cage?

You could say: "No. He has a room, called a cell, with a bed in it. The doors are locked at night. In the day he can come out, see other people and go outside."

What may be behind it: A scary picture in their head. Ask, "What do you think it looks like?" and then you can fix the bits that are wrong. Visiting My Dad in Prison has real photographs from inside Risdon.

Can I ring him?

You could say: "We can't ring him. He has to ring us. When he does, you can talk to him."

What may be behind it: They miss his voice. Calls can come at awkward times and end suddenly, which can upset a child. It helps to keep a list on the fridge of things to tell him, so they're ready when the phone goes.

How calls work, and what they cost, is in Stay Connected under "They ring you. You cannot ring them". Check there for the current rule.

Can I see him? Can I hug him?

You could say: "I'm going to find out. If we can visit, I'll tell you exactly what it will be like before we go."

Don't promise a visit or a hug until you know. Whether you can touch depends on the kind of visit, and that can change.

If they can't visit: "The rules say we can't visit right now. That is not because of anything you did. You can still write to him and draw for him."

What may be behind it: "Is he still my dad? Does he still want me?"

The kinds of visit, booking, and identification for children are in Stay Connected. Video visits may be possible if you live a long way away.

Can I send him something?

You could say: "Yes. You can write him a letter or do him a drawing, and we'll post it."

What may be behind it: Wanting to do something. Children feel better when there is a job they can do. Check the rules first, so they don't spend an afternoon on something that gets sent back. Nothing can usually be handed over at a visit.

Did he do something bad? Is he a bad person?

You could say:

  • Young children: "He did something that is against the law. That was wrong. He is still your dad and he still loves you."
  • Older children: "He did a bad thing. That doesn't mean he is all bad. People are more than the worst thing they've done."
  • If the court has not decided: "The police think he broke the law. The court is working that out."

The Practitioner's Guide suggests words like "good people sometimes do bad things" or "people make mistakes" (2018, p. 44).

What may be behind it: "Am I allowed to still love him?" The answer is yes. They are also allowed to be angry with him. Both at once is normal.

Am I bad like him?

You could say: "No. You are you. You make your own choices. Here are three things I love about you…"

What may be behind it: Shame, or something another child or adult has said. Children feel the judgment that falls on the family. Name the things they are good at, often.

Will the police take you too? Will they take me?

You could say: "No. I'm not going anywhere. I'm here, and I'm looking after you. Police don't take children to prison."

What may be behind it: If one parent can vanish, why not the other? This fear is strongest in children who saw the arrest. They may cling, or not want to go to school. Tell them each morning who is picking them up, and be there.

If your child saw the arrest and is having nightmares or is very frightened weeks later, talk to your GP or ring Kids Helpline with them.

What do I tell my friends? What if they find out?

You could say: "You get to choose. You can say 'That's private.' You can say 'My dad's away for a while.' Or you can tell a friend you really trust. None of those is a lie."

What may be behind it: Fear of being teased or left out. Practise the words together so they have them ready. If someone is unkind, they should tell you or a teacher. In Waiting for Dad, Josh tells his mate Max, and Max stays. It is a good one to read together.

Does he still love me? Does he think about me?

You could say: "Yes. Being in prison doesn't stop him loving you. He can't be here, and that is because of the rules, not because of you."

If he is not ringing or writing: "I don't know why he hasn't been in touch. I do know it is not because of you. Some people find it very hard to know what to say."

What may be behind it: "Did he leave because I'm not worth staying for?" Don't make promises on his behalf. Do keep telling them who loves them here and now.

Why did you lie to me?

For families who started with a different story.

You could say: "I told you he was away for work because I didn't know how to tell you the truth, and I wanted to protect you. That was a mistake. I'm sorry. I'll tell you the truth from now on."

What may be behind it: "Can I trust what you tell me now?" Say sorry once, plainly. Don't explain for too long. Then prove it by answering their next question honestly.

What happens at court? Can I come?

You could say: "Court is a place where people work out whether someone broke the law, and what should happen next. It can take a long time and there is a lot of waiting."

What may be behind it: Wanting to see him, or to know what is being decided about their own life. The court guide has a section, “Talking with children about court”, with words and pictures for this. Check with the court before taking a child.

What will it be like when he comes home?

You could say: "I don't know exactly. It might feel a bit strange at first, for all of us. We'll work it out slowly, and you can tell me how it's going."

What may be behind it: Excitement and worry at once. "Will he go away again? Will he be different? Will the rules at home change?" You can't promise how it will go. You can promise to keep listening.

Questions with no good answer

"Why did he do it?" "Why us?" "Will he do it again?" "Is he sorry?"

Sometimes there isn't an answer, or you don't have it.

You could say:

  • "I don't know. I wish I did."
  • "I wonder about that too."
  • "That's one you might want to ask him yourself one day. I can help you write it down."
  • "I can't answer that. But I can sit here with you."

A child who gets an honest "I don't know" learns they can keep asking you. A child who gets a made-up answer learns to stop.

If you're worried about how your child is going

Trouble sleeping, wetting the bed, anger, going quiet, not wanting to go to school: these are common when someone goes to prison, and they often settle when the child is told the truth and things at home feel steady.

If they don't settle, or you are frightened by something your child says:

  • Kids Helpline, 1800 55 1800. Free, any time, ages 5 to 25. They can ring or chat online.
  • Parentline Tasmania, 1300 808 178. For you.
  • 13YARN, 13 92 76. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, any time.
  • Lifeline, 13 11 14. Any time.
  • Onesimus Foundation, 0401 690 954.
  • Your GP, or your child's school.

If a child is in danger, call 000.
