Telling the children

"I didn't know when he was going to prison. I saw it in the newspaper and came home and found out."

14 year old girl, quoted in the Practitioner's Guide (2018), p. 32

Most people's first thought is to say nothing, to protect the child. This page is about why a small, true answer is usually kinder, and what words you can use tonight.

You do not have to do this perfectly. You only have to make sure your child is not left to work it out alone.

Should I tell them?

Usually, yes. Something small and true, and soon.

It is your choice. You know your child best.

Here is why most families find the truth works better than silence:

  • Children notice. They see you upset or stressed. They hear the phone calls. They know someone is missing.
  • They fill the gap. When nobody explains, children make up their own story. It is often worse than the truth. Many decide it is their fault.
  • They find out anyway. From other kids, the news, social media, or something they overhear. It is better coming from you.
  • You can stop pretending. Keeping a secret from a child is hard work. It is one less thing to carry.
  • They can get help. A child can only be comforted about something they are allowed to know.

The Practitioner's Guide puts it this way: "Children may create their own stories (that could be much worse) about why a family member is no longer there." (2018, p. 33)

You do not have to tell them everything. You do have to tell them something true.

Before you start

Pick a quiet time, keep it short, and expect to do it more than once.

  • Choose your moment. Somewhere familiar and private. Not just before school or bed if you can help it. Leave time afterwards for a cuddle or a question.
  • Work out your few sentences first. Say them out loud to yourself, or to a friend. Three sentences is enough to start.
  • Be ready for any reaction. Tears, anger, silence, or "Can I go and play now?" All of these are normal. Some children are relieved. That is normal too.
  • It is fine to say "I don't know." Follow it with "I will tell you as soon as I do."
  • It is fine to be upset yourself. You can say, "I'm sad about it too. We'll get through it together."
  • More than one child? Each one needs words that fit their age. You can tell them together and then talk to the older ones again on their own.
  • Expect it to come up again. In the car. At bedtime. In the shop. Weeks later. That is how children make sense of things. Answer it again each time.
Words you can borrow

Use your own words if you can. These are here for when you can't find any.

Swap in Mum, Pop, your brother, or whoever it is.

Under 5

One or two short sentences. Then keep the day as normal as you can.

  • "Dad can't live at home for now. He is staying at a place called prison."
  • "It is not because of anything you did."
  • "I am here. I am looking after you."

Very small children will not understand, but they feel the change. Keep talking about the person. Keep a photo where they can see it.

5 to 8

Simple facts, and room for questions.

  • "Dad broke a rule called a law. A law is a very important rule. When grown-ups break a law, sometimes they have to go to prison for a while."
  • "Prison is a place where he has to stay. He has a bed and food, and people there whose job is to keep everyone safe."
  • "He did not want to leave you. He loves you. This is not your fault."
  • "I don't know yet how long it will be. I will tell you when I know."

9 to 12

More detail. They will ask harder questions, and they may already know more than you think. Ask first: "What have you heard?"

  • "I want to tell you something true, because you are old enough to know. Dad has been sent to prison because he broke the law."
  • "The court decides how long. Right now it is … / Right now we don't know."
  • "You might feel angry, or sad, or embarrassed, or all of them. That's okay. You can still love him."
  • "You can ask me anything. If I don't know, I'll say so."

Teenagers

Be straight with them. They can look things up, and they will.

  • "I'd rather you heard this from me than from someone else."
  • "Here's what I know, and here's what I don't know yet."
  • "You get a say in this. Whether you visit, who you tell, how much you want to know."
  • "I'm not going to pretend it's fine. But we are going to be okay."

If they are waiting for court

  • "The police think Dad broke the law. A court is working out what happened. Until they decide, he has to stay in prison. We don't know yet what will happen."
What children most need to hear

Four things. Say them more than once.

  1. "It is not your fault." Nothing you did, said or thought made this happen.
  2. "You are loved." By me. And being in prison does not stop Dad loving you.
  3. "You will be looked after." Say who is picking them up, where they will sleep, and what stays the same: school, home, bedtime.
  4. "You can ask me anything." Now, or next week, or in the middle of the night.

It also helps to say: "The grown-ups are sorting this out. It is not your job to fix it."

If you have already told them something different

You can put it right. Children forgive a late truth far more easily than a long secret.

Plenty of families start with "he's working away" or "she's in hospital". You said it to protect them. That is nothing to be ashamed of.

You could say:

  • "Remember I said Dad was away for work? That wasn't true. I said it because I didn't know how to tell you. I'm sorry."
  • "The truth is Dad is in prison. I'm going to tell you the truth from now on."

They may be cross about the fib. Let them be. Say sorry once, simply, and then answer their questions.

If it is hard to say what happened

You can be truthful without giving every detail.

  • You don't have to explain the whole thing. "He hurt someone" or "He took things that weren't his" is true, and enough for a young child.
  • If it has been in the news or online, older children will probably see it. Tell them yourself first, in your words.
  • If the person in prison hurt you or the children, your child may feel relieved, or scared about them coming back, or still love them, or all three. Tell them: "It is okay to feel safe now. It is okay to miss him too."
  • If the person in prison has asked you not to tell, it is still your decision. You are the one at home with the child.
  • If you can't find the words at all, ring someone and talk it through first. See "Looking after yourself" below.
Who else to tell

You decide. You are not obliged to tell anyone.

  • School. You don't have to tell them. But a teacher who knows can quietly look out for your child, and is usually glad to be told. You can ask them to keep it private.
  • Family and close friends. Pick one or two people your child can also talk to. Children often hide their worries from you to protect you.
  • Help your child with what to say. They can choose. "That's private" is a full answer. So is "My dad's away at the moment." So is telling one friend they trust.
  • Private is not the same as secret. Private means it's our family's business and you choose who knows. Secret means you must never tell. Don't ask a child to keep a secret from everyone. It is too heavy.
If they are going to visit, or don't want to

Ask what they want. Never force a visit, and never promise one before you know it can happen.

  • Tell them the truth before a visit. A child who thinks they are going to "Dad's work" will be frightened by the gates and the search.
  • Walk them through it first. Read Visiting My Dad in Prison together. It shows a visit at Risdon step by step.
  • If they don't want to go, that's allowed. They can write, draw, or wait until they are ready. Ask again another time.
  • If visits are not allowed, tell them clearly that this is not their fault.

For booking, identification for children and what you can bring, see Stay Connected. The rules change, so check there or with the prison each time.

Looking after yourself while you do this

You are carrying a lot. You are allowed to get help with this conversation.

  • Say it to an adult first. A friend, your mum, a worker. The second time is easier.
  • You can ask someone to be there when you tell the children.
  • You won't get it perfect. If it comes out wrong, you can try again tomorrow. "I didn't say that very well yesterday. Can I have another go?"

People you can ring:

  • Parentline Tasmania, 1300 808 178. For parents and carers. Ring if you don't know what to tell the children.
  • Onesimus Foundation, 0401 690 954. We can talk it through with you.
  • Kids Helpline, 1800 55 1800. Free, any time, for anyone aged 5 to 25. Your child can ring or chat online themselves.
  • 13YARN, 13 92 76. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, any time.
  • Lifeline, 13 11 14. Any time, if it all gets too much.
