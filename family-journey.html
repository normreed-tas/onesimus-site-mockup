Someone you love has gone to prison.

This page walks you through what happens, step by step. You do not need to read all of it. Find where you are now and start there.

Nothing here is a judgement about you or about them. You have not done anything wrong, and you are allowed to ask for help.

Phase one

The first days

Step 1

They have been arrested

The police hold them first. If the police or a court let them out on bail, they come home and wait for court there.

If they are not let out, they are taken to a reception prison. This is a small prison where people go first.

  • Hobart Reception Prison — 27–29 Liverpool Street, Hobart
  • Launceston Reception Prison — 125–127 Cimitiere Street, inside the Launceston Police Station

Both have the same phone number: (03) 6165 8052.

What "bail" means

Bail means the court lets someone stay in the community while they wait for their court date. They usually have to follow rules, like living at a certain address.

What you can do now

  • Ring the reception prison and ask if they are there.
  • Expect staff to tell you very little. There are privacy rules. It is not personal, and it is not because of anything you have done.
  • Write down the date and anything you are told. You will be asked for it later.

Step 2

Waiting for court

What "remand" means

Remand means waiting. They have not been found guilty of anything yet. They are in prison because the court has not agreed to let them out until the case is heard.

People on remand can be in prison for weeks or months before a court decides anything.

Men on remand in the south are usually held at the Southern Remand Centre at Risdon Vale.

What you can do now

  • Find out who their lawyer is. If they do not have one, Legal Aid or a community legal service may be able to help.
  • Ask the lawyer when the next court date is. Court dates often move.
  • You can usually go to court and sit in the public seats. You do not have to.

Phase two

Finding out where they are

Step 3

Moved to Risdon

There are four prisons on one site at 672 East Derwent Highway, Risdon Vale.

  • Risdon Prison Complex — men, higher security
  • Ron Barwick Prison — men, lower security
  • Mary Hutchinson Women's Prison — women
  • Southern Remand Centre — men waiting for court

Which one they are in matters, because visits work differently at each one.

What happens when they arrive

  • A health check, and questions about their safety and wellbeing.
  • Their own clothes and belongings are put away. Any cash goes into a prison account.
  • Paperwork, and someone explains the rules and the daily routine.
  • They are given prison clothes and toiletries.
  • They are given a security rating, then moved to the right part of the prison.

Step 4

Where they will live in the prison

What "classification" means

Classification is the prison deciding two things. How closely someone needs to be supervised, and which part of the prison they will live in.

A group of staff makes the decision. They look at:

  • how long the sentence is
  • what the offence was, and any other charges
  • whether there is any risk to safety or security
  • how the person behaves
  • whether they join in with work, education and programs

This can change over time. If someone does well, it can change in their favour. That matters to you, because it can change what visits they are allowed.

Phase three

Staying in touch

Step 5

The first phone call

They have to ring you. You cannot ring them. This is the thing families find hardest at the start.

How they get you on their phone list

  • They can put up to five personal numbers on a list. They get five more for lawyers and professional people.
  • The prison will ring each number to check who you are, and to check that you are happy to take calls.
  • So answer calls from numbers you do not know for the first week or two. That call may be the prison.

Calls cost money. The money comes out of their prison account.

Putting money in their account

You can do this at Service Tasmania, at the Visitor Reception Centre, at the Hobart Reception Prison, or by bank cheque or Australia Post money order.

They can use it for phone credit and for the canteen.

Phase four

Visiting

Step 7

Becoming an approved visitor

You cannot just turn up. There are two steps, and both take time.

Step one: apply

  • Fill in a Visitor Application Form.
  • Put your children on the form too. If a child is not on your form, they cannot come with you.
  • If you want to bring a child who is not yours, you need a separate form and permission from their parent or guardian.

Step two: get registered at the prison

Once you are approved, the prison has to put you in their system before you can get through the doors.

That means a scan of your palm and a scan of your eye. The scans are how the doors know it is you. Every time you visit after that, you will scan again to get in.

What the scans are for

It is not a test and there is no right or wrong result. It just means the prison can be sure the person going in is the person who was approved. It takes a few minutes.

Children are not scanned. Only adults. Your child comes in with you, which is one of the reasons anyone under 18 has to be with an adult.

Step 8

Booking a visit

Book at least one day before, and no more than a week before.

Who to ring

There are only two numbers. Which one you use depends on where they are.

  • Risdon Vale site — Visitor Reception Centre, (03) 6165 7400, option 1.
    This covers the Risdon Prison Complex, Ron Barwick Prison, Mary Hutchinson Women's Prison and the Southern Remand Centre.
  • Hobart Reception Prison and Launceston Reception Prison — (03) 6165 8052.

Three kinds of visit

  • Contact visit — you sit at a table together. You can usually hug hello and goodbye.
  • Box visit — you talk through a glass window. You cannot touch.
  • Zoom visit — a video call, which you can do from home.

Which one you get depends on the prison, on their classification, and on how things are going for them.

A Zoom visit is worth asking about if you live a long way away, or if a long drive with small children is too much.

Check with TPS — current Zoom arrangements
Step 9

What happens when you get there

Get there early

Arrive at least 30 minutes before your visit time. If you get there later than that, you will be turned away and you will lose the visit.

Plan for traffic, parking, and a child who needs the toilet. Half an hour early is the latest you should aim for, not the earliest.

Then there is waiting, and there is a search before you go in.

  • You scan your palm and your eye, the same as when you registered. Children do not have to.
  • You hand in everything you are carrying. It goes in a locker, including jewellery.
  • You walk through a scanner that beeps if you have metal on you.
  • An officer may wave a hand-held scanner around you.
  • A dog may come and sniff you. You are not allowed to pat it.
  • Doors are heavy and they bang. It is loud. Children often find this the worst part.

None of this means anyone thinks badly of you. Every visitor goes through it.

Phase five

Your family

Step 10

Children

Bringing a child to a visit

  • Bring ID for every child, every single time. A Medicare card, a Healthcare or Concession card, or a birth certificate with their name on it.
  • Anyone under 18 has to come with an adult.
  • Babies can bring one bottle. No other food.
  • There are some toys and books in the visits area, but not many.
  • Plan to spend most of the visit playing with your children. If you need to talk about adult things, it is better to arrange care for them.
The two things that lose people a visit

No ID for a child, and arriving less than 30 minutes before. Families drive a long way and get turned away at the gate for both.

Put the cards in the car now. With children, leave even earlier than you think.

Your child will not be scanned. The palm and eye scans are for adults only, so there is nothing you need to prepare them for there.

Prison Service: children visiting

Telling the children

Most people's first instinct is to say nothing, to protect the child. Children almost always know something is wrong. When nobody tells them what it is, they usually imagine something worse, and often decide it is their fault.

You do not have to tell them everything. You do have to tell them something true.

We have two picture books you can read with a child — one about what a visit is actually like, and one for older children about telling a friend.

Step 11

Keeping your own household going

This is the part nobody warns you about, and for most families it is the hardest part of the first fortnight.

If the person who went to prison earned money, paid rent, drove the car or looked after the children, all of that lands on you at once, in the same week you are trying to work out where they are.

Worth sorting early

  • Rent or mortgage — tell them early rather than late
  • Centrelink — what you are entitled to may have changed
  • Bills and any loans in their name
  • The car, if it is in their name
  • School — you decide what to tell them, but a teacher who knows something is going on can help a child

If you are worried about them right now

If you think the person in prison is not safe, or they have said something that frightens you, tell the prison. You do not need proof and you will not get them into trouble for it.

  • Visitor Reception Centre — (03) 6165 7400, option 1
  • Hobart and Launceston Reception Prisons — (03) 6165 8052

If you are struggling yourself, that counts too. Lifeline is 13 11 14, any time of the day or night.
